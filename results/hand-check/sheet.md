## H01 - VRQ-001 on 2026-10-15

**Question:** It is 15 October 2026. I sent the official request form to keep a dog in my apartment three weeks ago. The agent said no because the owner is allergic, but the owner hardly ever visits. Can they refuse?

**Answer:**

> Based on the **Residential Tenancies Act 1997 (Vic)**, Authorised Version **v115** as of **13 October 2026** [6], here is how the law applies to your situation:
> 
> ### Can they refuse?
> **No, not unilaterally.** Under section 71C(1), a residential rental provider (the owner or their agent) must not unreasonably refuse consent to keep a pet [6]. 
> 
> If the owner wishes to refuse your request based on their allergy, they are legally required to apply to the Tribunal for an order stating that it is reasonable to refuse consent [6]. Crucially, under section 71C(2), if the provider does not make this Tribunal application **within 14 days** of receiving your official request form, they are automatically **taken to have consented** to you keeping the pet [6]. 
> 
> Because three weeks have passed and the agent simply said "no" without applying to the Tribunal within that 14-day window, the law considers your consent as already granted. The owner's allergy (and the fact that they hardly visit) would be a factor the Tribunal considers under "any other matter... relevant" [6], but it does not give them the right to unilaterally refuse after the statutory deadline has passed.
> 
> ### What you can do:
> 1. **Notify the agent in writing:** Inform the agent that because more than 14 days have passed without a Tribunal application, section 71C(2) deems their consent as granted. You are legally permitted to keep the dog. 
> 2. **Keep the pet:** You may proceed with keeping the dog on the premises under section 71A [6].
> 3. **Seek Tribunal confirmation if necessary:** If the agent continues to object or threatens eviction, you can apply to the Tribunal for a formal declaration confirming your right to keep the pet [6].

<details><summary>Gold answer</summary>

A renter asks to keep a pet using the official request form, and the landlord must not unreasonably refuse. The agent saying no is not enough to refuse: the landlord is taken to have consented unless they apply to VCAT within 14 days after getting the request. Only VCAT can decide that a refusal is reasonable, looking at things like the type of pet and the property; the owner's allergy doesn't decide it automatically. Three weeks have passed, so unless the landlord applied to VCAT within the first 14 days, consent is taken as given. VCAT also considers whether refusing is allowed under any other law, and because it's an apartment, the owners corporation rules may also cover pets; they are separate from the rental law. The renter can ask the agent in writing whether the landlord applied to VCAT within 14 days, and if not, say that consent is taken as given, and check the owners corporation rules before bringing the dog.

</details>

## H03 - VRQ-028 on 2026-10-15

**Question:** It is 15 October 2026. I live in a rooming house and the two washing machines are coin-operated. Are they allowed to charge us to use them?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** The retrieved documents don't say whether a rooming house operator can charge for coin-operated washing machines. They do set rules on charging for services, and they require a washing machine to be provided.
> 
> **What the law says**
> 
> - **Charges for utilities in your room:** An operator may charge for electricity, gas and water used in a room only if the operator pays the bill, the room is separately metered, and the charge is no more than the supplier's charge. This section does not apply to shared rooms [1]. It doesn't mention laundry or washing machines.
> - **Charging for services:** If an operator charges for services, they must give you a separate schedule of those charges before you move in, and an itemised account of your individual use if you use the services [1].
> - **Service charges by Homes Victoria or registered housing agencies:** These bodies may charge for laundry services only in declared rooming houses, and only where use can't practicably be measured. The charge must not exceed the cost of providing the service, and you can apply to the Tribunal to have it withdrawn or varied [1].
> - **Washing machines as a required facility:** The rooming house standards require, for every 12 or fewer residents, a laundry with a washing machine in good working order (version dated 1 December 2024, S.R. No. 10/2023) [2]. I can't confirm from the retrieved material that this is the current version.
> - **Unreasonable limits on utilities:** In a fixed term rooming house agreement, a term that unreasonably limits your use of utilities is a prohibited term [3].
> - **Breakdowns:** Urgent repairs include failure or breakdown of an essential appliance for laundering supplied by the operator, and the operator must carry them out after being notified [3].
> 
> **What you can do or ask**
> 
> 1. **Ask for the schedule of charges in writing.** Ask whether the coin machines are a charged service, and request the separate schedule and any itemised account the law requires [1].
> 2. **Check your agreement.** Look for any term about laundry or utilities. A fixed term agreement can't unreasonably limit your use of utilities [3].
> 3. **Report a broken machine.** If it breaks down, notify the operator. If they don't act, you may be able to arrange urgent repairs or apply to VCAT [3].
> 4. **Get specialist advice.** Consumer Affairs Victoria's renting information is at consumer.vic.gov.au/renting [3]. The documents don't say whether a coin-operated machine counts as a "service" under these rules, so confirm that with them or VCAT.

## H04 - VRQ-035 on 2025-10-01

**Question:** It is 1 October 2025. The photos in a rental ad look like they show mould in one of the bedrooms. Are they allowed to advertise it like that, and is the photo enough to prove the place doesn't meet the minimum standards?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** The Act's advertising ban may apply, but the version I can confirm for 1 October 2025 doesn't show it. The mould standard does apply, and a photo is good evidence but the documents don't say whether it is enough to prove a breach.
>

> 
> - The *Residential Tenancies Act 1997* version dated 6 August 2025 (file `rta-1997-v110-2025-08-06.pdf`) is the latest one dated before 1 October 2025 [4]. The extracts don't state the Authorised Version number directly, so I'm identifying it from the file label.
> - The *Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021* version dated 1 July 2025 (file `rt-regs-2021-v004-2025-07-01.pdf`) is the latest regulations version dated before that date [3].
> - Versions dated 25 November 2025 or later [1][5] postdate 1 October 2025, so I haven't relied on them for what applied that day.
> 
> ### What the law says
> 
> **Mould standard:** The regulations' rental minimum standards say "each room in the rented premises must be free from mould and damp caused by or related to the building structure" [3]. The 1 July 2025 version is the one in force on 1 October 2025.
> 
> **Duty to comply:** The provider must ensure rented premises comply with the rental minimum standards on or before the day the renter moves in [4, s 65A(1)].
> 
> **Advertising:** The later Act version says a provider or agent "must not advertise or otherwise offer premises to let unless" they "reasonably believe the premises comply with the rental minimum standards" at the time of advertising [1, s 65B]. However, the 6 August 2025 version I retrieved doesn't contain s 65B and only refers to ss 65, 68 and 70 in s 65A [4]. The documents don't give the commencement date for the amendment that added s 65B (No. 6/2025), so I can't confirm whether the advertising ban was in force on 1 October 2025.
> 
> **Records:** The later Act version requires providers who advertise to keep records "sufficient to demonstrate compliance" with s 65B [1, s 65E]. Again, this is from a version dated after 1 October 2025, so I can't confirm it applied then.
> 
> ### Is the photo enough?
> 
> The documents don't say what evidence proves a breach or how much weight a photo carries. They also don't say whether a photo of mould shows it is "caused by or related to the building structure", which the standard requires [3]. A photo may support a claim, but the documents don't say it proves non-compliance.
> 
> ### What the renter can do or ask
> 
> - **Ask for records:** Under the later version, the provider must keep records to show compliance with the advertising rule [1, s 65E]. I can't confirm that obligation applied on 1 October 2025.
> - **Request urgent repairs:** If the premises don't comply with the minimum standards on or just after the renter moves in, the renter "may issue a request to the residential rental provider for urgent repairs" [4, s 65A(2)].
> - **Rely on the general repair duty:** The provider must keep the premises "in good repair" and "in a reasonably fit and suitable condition for occupation" [4, s 68(1)]. This applies "whether or not the renter was aware of any disrepair" before moving in [4, s 68(1A)(a)].
>

> 
> - Whether the advertising ban (s 65B) was in force on 1 October 2025.
> - What evidence is needed to prove a breach of the mould standard.
> - How the mould standard is enforced or what steps the Tribunal would take.

[1] The rental provider may give **you** a notice to vacate under section 91ZV, specifying a termination date at least **14 days** after the notice is given. [2]
>

> 
> ## What you can do or ask
> 
> - Check whether your friend has exclusive use of part of the property, pays you rent, or otherwise has an arrangement that could amount to a sublet. The retrieved documents do not define how those facts determine whether a particular arrangement is a sublet.
> - If the friend’s conduct has caused a breach, VCAT can make a compliance order requiring you to remedy the breach and refrain from a further or similar breach. The Tribunal may also order that you do not permit the person responsible for the breach to enter or remain at the premises. [1]
> - If the rental provider applies for possession, VCAT must consider whether making a possession order is **reasonable and proportionate**, including whether the breach was caused by someone other than you and whether it has been remedied. [1]
> - Do **not** change the locks or use force based only on the documents retrieved. The documents state that only Victoria Police, acting on a VCAT possession order and warrant, can carry out a forcible eviction in the residential-rental context. [3]
> 
> The documents do not state whether you must notify the agent now, or give you a standalone notice/form for ending your friend’s occupation.

<details><summary>Gold answer</summary>

It depends on the arrangement, and the law doesn't give one answer without more facts. A rental agreement can be written or unwritten. If the renter let part of the home to the friend (for example, a room that is only theirs, in exchange for rent), that can be a sub-letting: the renter then counts as the friend's landlord under the Act and can only end it through the Act's notice process, and forcing a renter out other than under the Act is an offence. If the friend only shares the home without their own space, the Act may not apply and ordinary agreement rules decide it, which aren't in these documents. What decides it: whether the friend has a space that's only theirs, what was agreed, and what they pay. The renter shouldn't lock the friend out or remove their belongings. They should work out what was agreed and paid, and can get advice (for example, from Tenants Victoria or Consumer Affairs Victoria) before giving any notice.

</details>

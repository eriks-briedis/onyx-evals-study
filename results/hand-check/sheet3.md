## H302 - VRQ-022 on 2019-10-15

**Question:** This is happening now, on 15 October 2019. I signed a lease and picked up the keys, but before moving in I found damp, mould and rotting timber in the house. What are my options? Can I cancel, ask for repairs, or ask for lower rent?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** Under the law in force on 15 October 2019, you can likely cancel if you haven't moved in, and you can require repairs. The documents don't give you a right to reduce rent for these defects.
>
> **Governing version:** The *Residential Tenancies Act 1997*, Authorised Version No. 088 (file rta-1997-v088-2019-07-02.pdf, in force from 2 July 2019 to 3 December 2019), applies on your date [1]. It uses the newer terms "residential rental provider" (landlord) and "renter" (tenant) [1].
>
> ---
>
> ### What the law says
>
> - **Condition on move-in:** The provider must ensure the premises are vacant and in a reasonably clean condition on the agreed day you enter occupation. If they aren't, you are not required to move in, and you don't have to pay rent for the period before you actually move in [1]. This section is about cleanliness, so it doesn't clearly cover damp or rot.
> - **Minimum standards:** The provider must ensure the premises comply with prescribed rental minimum standards on or before the day you enter occupation (penalty: 60 penalty units for an individual, 300 for a body corporate). If they don't comply on or just after that day, you may request urgent repairs [1]. The documents do not include the content of these standards.
> - **Good repair:** The provider must keep the premises in good repair and reasonably fit and suitable for occupation. This applies even if you knew of the disrepair before moving in [1].
> - **Mould and damp:** "Urgent repairs" includes "the presence of mould or damp caused by or related to the building structure," and any fault that makes the premises unsafe or insecure [1]. The documents don't say whether rotting timber counts as unsafe.
>
> ---
>
> ### What you can do
>
> **1. Cancel**
> - If you have not entered possession and you give the landlord notice of termination on the ground that the premises are not in good repair or are unfit for human habitation, the tenancy terminates [1].
> - Check first whether picking up the keys counts as taking possession. If it does, this route may not apply.
> - The documents don't state a notice period for this termination.
>
> **2. Ask for repairs**
> - **Notify in writing:** Give written notice as soon as practicable after you become aware of the damage or breakdown [1]. If you receive a condition report, it counts as notice of any defects it states [1]. If you were not given one, you may complete one and give it to the provider within the period between inspecting the premises and 5 business days after moving in [1].
> - **Urgent repairs:** If you have taken reasonable steps to get the provider to repair immediately and they can't, you may arrange the repairs. The provider must reimburse the reasonable cost within 7 days after written notice of the repairs and their cost [1].
> - **Urgent repairs at the Tribunal (VCAT):** You can apply if you can't meet the cost, the repairs cost more than the prescribed amount, or the provider refuses to pay. The Tribunal must hear the application within 2 business days [1].
> - **Non-urgent repairs:** If repairs aren't done within 14 days after your written notice, you can apply to VCAT for an order requiring specified repairs. The Tribunal must hear the application within 7 days and may order that a suitably qualified person carry out the work [1].
> - **Rent into the Rent Special Account:** If you have given notice and the provider has failed to carry out repairs, you may apply for an order that your rent be paid into the Rent Special Account. The Tribunal must make the order unless the provider shows financial hardship [1].
>
> **3. Ask for lower rent**
> - The documents do not contain a provision allowing you to reduce rent because of these defects.
> - The closest provisions are the no-rent-before-move-in rule described above [1] and the Director's power to investigate rent that is excessive, which is tied to reduced services or a proposed rent increase [1]. Neither clearly provides a rent reduction in your situation.
> - Holding rent in the Rent Special Account [1] is not a reduction.
>
> ---
>
> **Next steps:** Put your complaint in writing now, with photos, and keep a copy. Then decide whether to cancel or require repairs, and consider taking advice from VCAT or Consumer Affairs Victoria before acting. This summary is based only on the Act text retrieved and is not legal advice.

## H303 - NEWSQ-008 on 2025-10-01

**Question:** It is 1 October 2025. I applied for a rental at the advertised price, then got an email saying I had withdrawn even though I had not. The property is now listed at a higher rent, and I heard someone else offered more than the advertised price. Can I challenge what happened, and what should I save as evidence?

**Answer:**

> **Version applied:** *Residential Tenancies Act 1997*, Authorised Version No. 110 (file "rta-1997-v110-2025-08-06.pdf", dated 6 August 2025). It incorporates amendments as at 6 August 2025 [1]. Its metadata shows it was in force until 24 November 2025, so it covers 1 October 2025. The retrieved documents don't say it updates or replaces any other document.
>
> ## What the law says
>
> - **Fixed rent only.** A rental provider or their agent must not advertise or offer premises unless the rent is advertised or offered as a fixed amount (s 30F(1)) [1].
> - **No inviting higher offers.** They must not "solicit or otherwise invite an offer of an amount of rent that is higher than the advertised amount" (s 30F(3)) [1].
> - **Penalty.** Each of these breaches carries 60 penalty units for a natural person and 300 for a body corporate [1].
> - **False or misleading rent advertising.** A provider or agent who promotes or advertises rented premises must not make a false or misleading representation about the rent (s 30G(2)). This section applies only to providers and agents not acting in trade or commerce, and it doesn't limit the Australian Consumer Law (Victoria) [1].
> - **Misleading conduct.** A provider or agent must not induce someone to enter a rental agreement through misleading or deceptive conduct. This includes false or misleading representations about the rent payable (s 30E(2)–(3)(b)). It has the same trade-or-commerce limit, and it also doesn't limit the Australian Consumer Law (Victoria) [1]. A note to s 30E says s 472(1A) allows Tribunal orders on an application about a contravention of that section [1].
>
> ## What the documents don't settle
>
> - **The false "withdrawn" email.** The retrieved text has no provision that specifically deals with wrongly recording an application as withdrawn. You could argue it was misleading conduct under s 30E, or part of a scheme to invite higher offers under s 30F(3). That depends on facts I don't have, such as who sent the email and why.
> - **The competing offer.** Section 30F(3) bans *soliciting or inviting* higher offers. The retrieved text doesn't say what happens if another applicant offers more without being invited. So whether the provider acted unlawfully depends on whether they invited or encouraged the higher offer.
> - **The higher relisting.** The retrieved text doesn't expressly ban relisting at a higher rent. It only requires a fixed advertised amount and bans inviting offers above it [1].
> - **Tribunal powers and procedure.** The documents don't set out the text of s 472, the Tribunal's available orders, or any time limits.
>
> ## What you can do
>
> 1. **Challenge it at the Tribunal.** On the retrieved text, a s 30E misleading-conduct application is the clearest route. Ask the Tribunal what orders are available, because that detail isn't in the retrieved documents [1].
> 2. **Ask the provider or agent in writing** why you were recorded as withdrawn. Ask them to confirm whether any higher offer was invited.
> 3. **If you paid a holding deposit**, s 50 requires it to be refunded. If an agreement is entered into within 14 days, the refund is due when it is entered into. If not, it is due on the next business day after the 14 days [1].
> 4. **Check whether discrimination could be a factor.** Refusing to let on a protected attribute is separately prohibited (s 30A), and s 210AA allows a compensation application [1].
>
> ## What to save as evidence
>
> The documents don't list evidence requirements. These suggestions are based on what the provisions turn on:
>
> - The **original advertisement** showing the advertised rent, with screenshots and dates.
> - The **relisting** at the higher rent, also with screenshots and dates.
> - Your **application and any confirmation** you received.
> - The **"withdrawn" email**, with full headers, sender and timestamp. Also keep anything showing you never withdrew, such as your sent messages or call logs.
> - **All messages with the agent or provider**, including anything suggesting offers above the advertised price were welcome.
> - **Proof of any payment**, such as a holding deposit or application fee.
> - Any **record of who told you about the higher offer**, and when.
>
> If you want a firm view on remedies and time limits, check s 472 of the Act and consider contacting Consumer Affairs Victoria or a community legal service. The documents I retrieved don't cover those points.

**Check (must-mention):** passes only if the answer states every point:

- Relisting at a higher fixed rent isn't illegal by itself.
- Asking for or inviting offers above the advertised rent is banned.
- If the higher offer was unsolicited, the rental Act did not ban accepting it on this date (only inviting it was banned).
- The false "withdrawn" email isn't covered by a specific rental-law rule.

## H304 - VRQ-004 on 2025-10-01

**Question:** It is 1 October 2025. My agent emailed to say the rent is going up. I tried to negotiate and they said no, but I never got an official rent increase notice. Do I have to pay the higher rent, and from when?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** Based on the documents, an agent's email saying the rent is going up is probably not enough. The law requires a formal notice, and a rent increase made without one is invalid. Your agent may still send a valid notice later, so you should check any notice you receive.
>
> Today is 1 October 2025, so the applicable versions are the *Residential Tenancies Act 1997* (Authorised Version No. 110, in force from 6 August 2025) [1] and the *Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021* (Authorised Version No. 004, in force from 1 July 2025) [2].
>
> ## What the law says
>
> - **Notice is required.** A rental provider "must give a renter at least 60 days notice in the prescribed form of a proposed rent increase" [1].
> - **Required content.** The notice must state the amount of the increase, the method used to calculate it, and your right to apply to the Director within 30 days [1].
> - **One increase per notice, once every 12 months.** A notice may provide for only one increase [1], and rent cannot be increased at intervals of less than 12 months [1].
> - **Fixed terms.** During a fixed term, rent can only increase if the agreement provides for it [1].
> - **Invalid increases.** "A rent increase in contravention of this section is invalid" [1].
>
> ## What you can do
>
> - **Check whether you received a valid notice.** The prescribed form is Form 5, which states that the notice must be given at least 60 days before the increase and must include the method used to calculate it. The form also says the rental provider's name cannot be the agent's name [2].
> - **Apply to the Director.** If you receive a valid notice and think the proposed rent is excessive, you can apply in writing to the Director of Consumer Affairs Victoria within 30 days after the notice is given [1][2].
> - **Apply to VCAT.** After the Director's report, you may apply to VCAT within 30 days of receiving it. You can also apply directly to VCAT, with leave, if you have reasonable grounds for not requesting a report first [1].
> - **Pay the increase during a challenge.** Form 5 states that you will need to pay the increased rent from the date on the notice unless VCAT decides otherwise [2].
>
> ## What the documents don't cover
>
> - **Effective date:** The documents don't say what date a rent increase takes effect if no valid notice was given. The only start date in the documents is the one stated on a valid notice [2].
> - **Whether an email counts:** The documents don't say whether an agent's email could ever satisfy the notice requirement. They do say the notice must be in the prescribed form [1][2].
> - **Your specific agreement:** I don't have your tenancy agreement, so I can't check whether it has a fixed term or a built-in increase clause.
>
> For help, Form 5 directs renters to the renting section of the Consumer Affairs Victoria website (consumer.vic.gov.au/renting) or 1300 558 181 [2].

**Check (must-mention):** passes only if the answer states every point:

- A rent increase needs notice on the official form (Form 5).
- At least 60 days notice.
- The email without the form is not a valid notice, so the higher rent isn't payable yet (an increase that breaks the rules is invalid).
- At least one next step: keep paying the current rent, tell the agent in writing, or ask Consumer Affairs Victoria to investigate once a proper notice arrives.

## H306 - VRQ-011 on 2026-01-15

**Question:** It's the middle of January 2026 (Thursday the 15th). My rent app charges a fee when I pay by card. There's also a manual bank transfer option with no fee, but it's slower. Can the app charge me the card fee? Does it matter whether the fee goes to the app company or to the agent?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** Based on the documents, your landlord or agent probably can't make you pay a card fee when a fee-free option, such as bank transfer, is reasonably available. The documents don't say whether the fee goes to the app company or the agent, and that difference doesn't appear to change the rule.
>
> **Versions in force:** On 15 January 2026, the *Residential Tenancies Act 1997* (rta-1997-v111-2025-11-25.pdf, dated 25 November 2025, version 111) [1] and the *Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021* (rt-regs-2021-v005-2025-11-25.pdf, dated 25 November 2025, version 005) [2] were both in force. I'm relying on the version numbers in the documents' metadata.
>
> **What the law says**
>
> - The provider or agent "must ensure that a rent payment method that incurs no additional costs (other than bank fees or account fees payable on the renter's bank account) is reasonably available to the renter" [1]. The penalty is 80 penalty units for a natural person or 400 for a body corporate [1].
> - Before you agree to a payment method, the provider or agent must tell you about any costs you may incur, including "third party transaction fees" and "any other electronic payment facility fees" [1].
> - The provider must allow payment by Centrepay and by a prescribed method [1]. The prescribed method is electronic funds transfer [2].
> - A term in a residential rental agreement that requires the renter to pay rent in advance by a payment method that has additional costs (other than bank or account fees) is a prohibited term [2].
> - Schedule 1 of the regulations notes that the provider "must permit at least one fee-free (other than the renter's own bank fees) payment method" [2].
>
> **Does it matter who gets the fee?**
>
> The documents don't distinguish between a fee paid to the app company and one paid to the agent. The obligations in section 42(4) fall on the provider "or that person's agent" [1]. The documents don't address whether an app company acting for the agent changes this. I can't confirm that from these documents.
>
> **A rule that doesn't yet apply:** Regulation 21C bans rent payment fees, except payment surcharges permitted under another Act, but it "has effect on and after 31 March 2026" [2]. It is not in force on 15 January 2026. The documents also don't include the text of section 51A of the Act, which it refers to [2].
>
> **What you can do**
>
> - Ask the provider or agent to confirm in writing that a no-fee method, such as bank transfer, is available, and that you can use it without paying the card fee.
> - Ask for the cost information the law requires them to give you before you consent to the card option [1].
> - If you pay by bank transfer, you can request a written receipt. For a payment not made in person, it must be given "within 5 business days of receiving the payment" [1].
>
> The documents don't describe how to make a complaint or what the Tribunal process is, so I can't advise on those steps from these sources.

- A payment method with no extra cost (apart from the renter's own bank fees) must be reasonably available, and the slower fee-free bank transfer may satisfy that.
- The ban on rent-payment fees is not yet in effect on this date: the list of banned fees (reg 21C) applies from 31 March 2026. (Saying the specific fee ban does not yet apply, without the date, also counts.)

## H307 - NEWSQ-005 on 2019-11-01

**Question:** It is 1 November 2019. I followed up repairs to leaking fixtures and got a notice to vacate the same day. I am renting month to month. What should I check if I think the notice is retaliation for asking for repairs?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** The retrieved text does not contain a general rule that makes a notice to vacate void because it followed a repair request. It does give you a 30-day window to challenge a notice at the Tribunal, and it sets out formal requirements the notice must meet.
>
> **Document used:** *Residential Tenancies Act 1997*, Authorised Version No. 088 (in force from 2 July 2019 to 3 December 2019), which covers 1 November 2019 [1].
>
> ## What the law says
>
> - **Formal validity:** A notice to vacate is not valid unless it is in the prescribed form, addressed to the renter, signed by the provider or their agent, and specifies the reasons and the termination date [1]. For some grounds it must also be accompanied by supporting documentary evidence [1].
> - **Challenging the notice:** A renter who receives a notice to vacate under sections 91ZX, 91ZY, 91ZZ, 91ZZA, 91ZZB or 91ZZC may apply to the Tribunal to challenge its validity. The application must be made within 30 days after the notice is given [1]. If the notice was given on 1 November 2019, that period would end around 1 December 2019. Please confirm the exact count.
> - **Retaliation-related protections in the text:** The only "no effect" provisions I found that refer to exercising a renter's rights are for:
>   - a threats-and-intimidation notice (s 91ZK), which is of no effect if given in response to the renter exercising a right under the Act [1]; and
>   - an end-of-fixed-term notice (s 91ZZD), which is of no effect if given in response to the renter exercising a right under the Act or making a report under s 72AA [1].
>   
>   Neither covers a notice based on repairs, and s 91ZZI(1) applies only to fixed-term agreements [1]. Because you are month to month, those fixed-term rules probably don't apply to you.
> - **Discrimination:** A notice under the repair/renovation-type sections is of no effect if giving it would be direct discrimination under the Equal Opportunity Act 2010 [1].
>
> ## Your repair rights
>
> - The renter must give written notice to the provider as soon as practicable after becoming aware of a breakdown of facilities or fixtures [1]. Check whether your repair request was in writing.
> - If non-urgent repairs are not carried out within 14 days of written notice, you may apply to the Director to investigate . The Tribunal can also be asked to order repairs .
> - For urgent repairs, you may arrange them if you took reasonable steps to get the provider to do them and they could not be reached . The provider must reimburse reasonable costs within 7 days of written notice .
>
> ## What to check
>
> 1. **The stated reason.** Which ground is given? Repair-related retaliation claims are hard to establish under a ground that is not in the Act or is unsupported.
> 2. **Form and signature.** Is it on the prescribed form, signed, addressed to you, and does it state a reason and a termination date [1]?
> 3. **Termination date.** The notice periods vary by ground. Compare the date given with the requirements in the relevant section.
> 4. **Supporting evidence.** If the ground requires documents, were they attached [1]?
> 5. **Timing.** Diary the 30-day deadline to apply to the Tribunal [1].
> 6. **Your written repair requests.** Keep copies and dates, since they show the sequence of events.
>
> ## Gaps
>
> The retrieved material does not include a general anti-retaliation provision for a notice to vacate on a repair-based or month-to-month ground. The search also did not return the full Act, so I cannot confirm there is no such provision elsewhere. For advice on your specific notice, contact the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal or a tenants' advice service.

## H308 - VRQ-028 on 2026-10-15

**Question:** It is 15 October 2026. I live in a rooming house and the two washing machines are coin-operated. Are they allowed to charge us to use them?

**Answer:**

> **Short answer:** The documents I retrieved don't say directly whether a rooming house can charge for coin-operated washing machines. They do set out rules on charges and on what a washing machine must be provided, so the answer depends on who runs your house and what your agreement says.
>
> ## Version of the law applied
>
> The Act in force on 15 October 2026 is the *Residential Tenancies Act 1997* (No. 109 of 1997), file version 115, which took effect on 13 October 2026 [1]. I can't confirm a separate "Authorised Version No." beyond this version number.
>
> ## What the law says
>
> - **Utility charges (s 108):** An operator may charge for electricity, gas and water only if the operator pays for them, the room is separately metered, and the charge is no more than the supplier's charge [1]. This doesn't mention washing machines.
> - **Schedule and itemised account (s 109):** If an operator charges for any services, it must give you a separate schedule of those charges before you move in, and an itemised account of your individual use if you use them [1].
> - **Service charges by public housing bodies (s 109A):** Homes Victoria or a registered housing agency may charge for laundry services only in a declared rooming house, and only if it isn't possible or practicable to measure your use. The charge must not exceed the cost of providing the service. They must give written notice of the charge, and you can apply to the Tribunal to have it withdrawn or varied [2]. This doesn't apply to private operators.
> - **Washing machines must be provided (Regulations):** From 26 February 2024, a rooming house must provide, for every 12 or fewer residents, a washing machine in good working order, plus a laundry trough and a clothes drying facility [2].
> - **Unreasonable limits on utilities:** A rooming house agreement term that "unreasonably limits the resident's use of utilities" is a prohibited term under the *Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021* (S.R. No. 3/2021), as in the version in force from 30 June 2026 [3]. The documents don't say whether a coin charge would count as unreasonable.
>
> ## What you can do or ask
>
> 1. **Check your agreement and the schedule of services.** Under s 109, the operator should have given you a separate schedule of any service charges before you moved in [1].
> 2. **Ask who owns the house.** If it's run by Homes Victoria or a registered housing agency, s 109A's limits apply, and you can apply to the Tribunal over a laundry charge [2].
> 3. **Ask for an itemised account** if you're charged for use of the machines [1].
> 4. **Ask whether the machines are working.** The washing machine standard requires them to be in good working order [2].
> 5. **Consider whether the charge is unreasonable.** If it seems to unreasonably limit your use of the machines, that may fall under the prohibited-term rule [3]. The documents don't set out how to challenge it, so you may want to get advice from a tenancy service.

## H309 - NEWSQ-005 on 2019-11-01

**Question:** It is 1 November 2019. I followed up repairs to leaking fixtures and got a notice to vacate the same day. I am renting month to month. What should I check if I think the notice is retaliation for asking for repairs?

**Answer:**

> On 1 November 2019 the law that applied was the **Residential Tenancies Act 1997, Authorised Version 088** (document *rta-1997-v088-2019-07-02.pdf*, dated 2 July 2019, in force 2 July 2019 to 3 December 2019) [1].
>
> ---
>
> ## What the law said
>
> **1. A "no reason" notice given in retaliation has no effect.** A landlord could give a notice to vacate without giving a reason under **s 263** [1]. But a notice under s 261 or s 263 "is of no effect if it was given in response to the exercise, or proposed exercise, by the tenant of a right under this Act" (**s 266(2)**) [1].
>
> **2. Asking for repairs is a right under the Act.** The landlord had to keep the premises "in good repair" (**s 68(1)**) [1]. The Act gave you these repair rights:
> - arranging urgent repairs yourself after taking reasonable steps to get the landlord to do them (**s 72**) [1]
> - applying to the Tribunal for urgent repairs (**s 73**) [1]
> - applying to the Director for an investigation after giving written notice and waiting 14 days (**s 74**) [1]
> - applying to the Tribunal for non-urgent repairs (**s 75**) [1]
> - asking to pay rent into the Rent Special Account (**s 77**) [1]
>
> **3. Notice period for a month-to-month tenancy.** A s 263 notice had to give a termination date "not less than 120 days after the date on which the notice is given" (**s 263(2)(c)**). If your periodic tenancy fell under s 230(3A), the period was the prescribed period, or 120 days if none was prescribed [1].
>
> **4. Strict time limit to challenge.** You could not apply to the Tribunal to challenge a s 263 notice "after the end of 60 days after the date on which the notice is given" (**s 266(3)(c)**) [1].
>

## H311 - VRQ-003 on 2025-10-01

**Question:** It is 1 October 2025. Our landlord gave us a notice to vacate because they were selling. The buyer pulled out, but the landlord hasn't withdrawn the notice. Do we still have to leave?

**Answer:**

> ## What the law says
>
> The law I'm applying is the **Residential Tenancies Act 1997**, file "rta-1997-v110-2025-08-06.pdf" (dated 6 August 2025). Its metadata labels it version 110 and shows it in force from 6 August 2025 to 24 November 2025, so it covers 1 October 2025 [1]. The extracts I retrieved don't print the words "Authorised Version No.", so version 110 comes from the metadata.
>
> - **The notice stays in effect until it is formally withdrawn.** A notice to vacate is withdrawn only if a notice of withdrawal is given. That notice must be in writing and signed by both the person who gave the notice and the person who received it [1, s 91ZZQ]. The landlord doing nothing, or the buyer pulling out, doesn't withdraw it. The retrieved text says nothing about a notice lapsing automatically when a sale falls through.
> - **You don't have to leave just because the notice exists.**
>   - The agreement ends if you move out on or after the termination date in the notice [1, s 91E].
>   - A landlord can't require or try to compel you to leave except as the Act allows. Breaching this carries a penalty of 150 penalty units for a natural person [1, s 91P].
>   - If you stay, the landlord can apply to VCAT for a possession order [1, s 322(1)]. That application can be made any time after the notice is given, up to 30 days after the termination date [1, s 326(1)].
>   - VCAT can't decide the application before the termination date [1, s 329].
> - **What VCAT must find before ordering you out.** Among other things, it must be satisfied that the landlord was entitled to give the notice and that the notice has not been withdrawn. It must also be satisfied that making the order is reasonable and proportionate, taking into account your interests and the landlord's [1, s 330(1)(a), (f)].
> - **Does the failed sale affect the landlord's entitlement?** A sale notice is allowed where the premises are, immediately after the termination date, "to be sold or offered for sale with vacant possession". It is also allowed within 14 days of a contract of sale being entered into or becoming unconditional. It must give at least 60 days' notice [1, s 91ZZB]. The documents don't say whether a buyer withdrawing makes the notice invalid. That is likely to depend on whether the landlord still intends to sell or offer the property for sale with vacant possession. I can't confirm that from the documents.
>
> ## What you can do or ask
>
> 1. **Ask the landlord to withdraw the notice in writing.** Both you and the landlord must sign it for the withdrawal to count [1, s 91ZZQ].
> 2. **Challenge the notice at VCAT.** On or before the hearing of a possession application, you can apply to challenge the notice's validity. The application must be made within 30 days after the notice was given [1, s 91ZZS]. VCAT can decide whether the notice is valid [1, s 91ZZT]. Check the date on your notice, because that window may already be closing or closed.
> 3. **If a hearing happens, argue it shouldn't result in an order.** You can raise that the order wouldn't be reasonable or proportionate in your circumstances [1, s 330(1)(f)]. The sale falling through is a fact VCAT can weigh under its other relevant matters, though the documents don't list it specifically.
> 4. **If you want to leave early,** you can give a notice of intention to vacate on 14 days' notice after a sale-related notice, without waiting for the termination date [1, s 91ZB(1)(e), (2), (3)]. The prescribed notice-to-vacate form says the same for "the premises are to be sold" notices [2, Form 6, cl 5]. That form is in the Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021, "rt-regs-2021-v004-2025-07-01.pdf" (dated 1 July 2025, version 004, in force until 24 November 2025) [2].
> 5. **Know the 6-month rule.** If the landlord gets possession under a sale notice, they must not re-let the premises as a residence for 6 months after the notice date. The penalty is 150 penalty units for a natural person. Exceptions apply, such as the landlord's own family moving in or VCAT permitting the letting [1, s 91ZZH].
>
> **In short:** the notice remains valid until formally withdrawn in writing, but you can only be made to leave by a VCAT possession order. It would be sensible to get advice from a community legal service or Consumer Affairs Victoria. The prescribed form points to them for this [2].

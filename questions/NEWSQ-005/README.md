# NEWSQ-005

**Role:** date-sensitive  
**Set:** main (one of the 10 questions the study was developed on)

## Situation

A renter on a periodic (month-to-month) agreement in regional Victoria followed up leaking fixtures and received a notice to vacate within hours. The agent denied retaliation. The notice is given on the scenario date, and the renter asks straight away.

## Question

I followed up repairs to leaking fixtures and got a notice to vacate the same day. I am renting month to month. What should I check if I think the notice is retaliation for asking for repairs?

**Boundary:** 29 Mar 2021: no-reason notices (s 263) abolished (and barred by COVID-19 measures from 29 Mar 2020)

## Gold — scenario date 2019-11-01

Status: approved 26 October 2026.

**Must cite (version in force on this date):**
- Residential Tenancies Act 1997, Authorised Version No. 088 (ss 263, 266(2)–(3))

**Correct answer (short):** On a month-to-month lease, a landlord can give a notice to vacate without giving a reason, with at least 120 days notice. But a no-reason notice has no effect if it was given in response to the tenant using, or proposing to use, a right under the Act, such as asking for repairs. The tenant should check whether the notice states a reason, the notice period, and the timing against the repair requests, and keep records of those requests. To challenge the notice, the tenant can apply to VCAT within 60 days after it is given.

**Must mention:**
- A no-reason notice is allowed, with at least 120 days notice.
- A no-reason notice has no effect if given in response to the tenant using a right (such as asking for repairs).
- The tenant can challenge it at VCAT within 60 days.

**Must not say:**
- That the landlord needed to give a reason.
- A deadline other than 60 days for challenging this notice at VCAT.
- A wrong version of the Act.

## Gold — scenario date 2026-10-15

Status: approved 26 October 2026.

**Must cite (version in force on this date):**
- Residential Tenancies Act 1997, Authorised Version No. 115 (ss 91ZZO, 91ZZI(3)–(4), 91ZZS–91ZZT, 330(1), 330A)

**Correct answer (short):** A landlord can't end a lease without a reason. The notice to vacate must be on the official form, state a reason allowed by the Act, and, for some reasons (such as sale, major repairs, demolition, or the owner or their family moving in), come with supporting evidence. The Act has no general rule that a retaliatory notice is void; its express rules cover only a few notice types (threats and intimidation, and eligibility notices in NRAS or affordable-housing tenancies). The renter should check the stated reason, the evidence and the dates, and keep records of the repair requests. If the notice was given for one of the evidence-backed reasons (sale, repairs, demolition, business use, the owner moving in, or public purposes), they can challenge it at VCAT within 30 days after it is given; for other reasons, they can challenge it when the landlord applies for a possession order. If the landlord applies to evict, VCAT must be satisfied the landlord was entitled to give the notice and that eviction is reasonable and proportionate, and it considers the landlord's or agent's behaviour, so the renter can raise retaliation there.

**Must mention:**
- The notice must state a lawful reason, on the official form, with supporting evidence for some reasons (such as sale or repairs).
- No general rule makes a retaliatory notice void.
- At least one next step: challenge at VCAT within 30 days (for the evidence-backed reasons), or raise retaliation at the eviction hearing.

**Must not say:**
- That a retaliatory notice is automatically void.
- That a landlord can give a no-reason notice.
- That the deadline for a direct VCAT challenge is something other than 30 days.
- A wrong version of the Act.

**Pass / fail rule:** Pass if the answer covers everything under "must mention", names the document and version it relies on (from the "must cite" list), and says nothing on the "must not say" list. Otherwise fail.
